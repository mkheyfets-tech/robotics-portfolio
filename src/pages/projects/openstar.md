## Understanding the people around a new technology

As part of WPI's Interactive Qualifying Project, I worked with a team and OpenStar Technologies to study public attitudes toward fusion energy in New Zealand. The goal was to understand people's questions and concerns, then use that evidence to inform public outreach.

The project looked beyond the technical promise of fusion to ask how people want to be included in decisions about their energy future.

## How we approached the research

We combined public surveys with interviews involving residents and experts in engineering, policy, academia, and the energy sector. Survey responses supported statistical analysis, while interview coding helped identify recurring themes in people's own words.

A survey experiment compared responses to the terms **“fusion” and “nuclear fusion.”** In that sample, the wording did not produce a statistically significant difference in support or perceived safety.

## What emerged

Respondents raised questions about cost, financial feasibility, transparency, and participation in decision-making. Greater familiarity with fusion was associated with more positive views, though that association alone does not establish why attitudes differed.

A recurring theme was a gap between caring about the energy future and feeling able to influence it. That pointed toward communication that invites questions and participation, alongside explaining the technology.

## From findings to recommendations

The team developed an evidence-based communications policy for OpenStar. Recommendations included two-way feedback tools, resources for community ambassadors, and messages tailored to different audiences.

The work broadened my view of engineering: introducing a technology also means understanding the people who may use it, live alongside it, or help decide its future.
